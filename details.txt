You're right, and it's a fair objection. Bug-finding has a correct answer sitting in the code, so a single strong model can retrieve it. That whole class of project is now commoditized.

Simulation is the correct instinct, for a specific reason worth being able to articulate in the interview:

> In a crew/pipeline, agents are just prompts in sequence — one model can do the same work. In a simulation, agents hold **private state and conflicting objectives**, so the result is *emergent*. There is no answer to retrieve. You have to run the system to find out. That's when multi-agent stops being decoration and becomes load-bearing.

Then the second half, which is what makes it CV-grade instead of a toy: **replicate a known result, then run an ablation that was impossible before LLM agents.**

## The pick: Bullwhip — LLM agents playing the Beer Distribution Game

Four agents in a supply chain: Retailer → Wholesaler → Distributor → Factory. Each sees **only** the orders from the guy directly downstream and their own inventory. Nobody sees real customer demand except the Retailer. Shipments take 2 turns to arrive.

Customer demand is boring on purpose: steady at 8 units/week, then a single one-time step up to 12. That's it. That's the entire shock.A 4-unit blip at the customer end turns into wild 40-unit swings at the factory. Every agent behaves reasonably; the system behaves insanely. That gap is the whole point — and it's measurable as an **amplification ratio** (variance of factory orders ÷ variance of customer demand).

**Why this can't be replaced by "ask Claude Code"**: there's no answer sitting anywhere to retrieve. The number only exists after you run 4 agents × 40 turns with restricted information. Claude Code can write the harness. It can't tell you what the amplification ratio was — that's your experimental result.

**Then the ablation, which is the part that makes it yours:**

| Condition | What changes | What you measure |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Agents see only downstream orders | Amplification ratio |
| Shared visibility | All agents see real customer demand | Does bullwhip collapse? |
| Chat channel | Agents send free-text messages upstream | Does talking help, or do they panic each other? |
| Personality | One agent prompted as "risk-averse" | Does a single anxious link poison the chain? |

The last two are only possible because the agents are LLMs. Classic agent-based models used fixed formulas — they couldn't panic, negotiate, or lie. That's a genuine gap you're filling, and it's an honest one to claim in an interview.

**Scope:** the engine is maybe 150 lines — integer inventory, a 2-turn shipping delay, a backlog counter. Agents return `{"order": N, "reasoning": "..."}`. Run 40 weeks, log every decision to JSON, plot with matplotlib. Weekend build, and the reasoning log is gold: you'll catch an agent literally writing *"my supplier is slow, I'll double my order to be safe"* — that sentence *is* the bullwhip, in the agent's own words. Put that quote in your README.

**Two alternates, same principle:**

- **Double auction market.** 8 buyers/8 sellers, each with a private valuation nobody else sees, trading through a deterministic order book. Known result: prices converge to theoretical equilibrium within a few rounds even with very few traders (Vernon Smith, 1962 — it won a Nobel). You compute equilibrium from the supply/demand curves, then show your LLM traders finding it. Chart of price converging to the dotted line is a beautiful demo.
- **Braess's paradox.** Commuter agents pick routes each round. You add a new fast road — and average travel time gets *worse*. Counterintuitive, tiny to build, and watching agents rediscover it is a great 60-second story.

**CV line:**

> Multi-agent supply chain simulation (CrewAI) reproducing the bullwhip effect from information asymmetry alone; measured 6.2× order-variance amplification across four autonomous agents, eliminated under shared-demand-visibility ablation.

That reads like a result, not a tutorial. The difference between "I built a crew" and "I ran an experiment and got a number" is most of the gap between a CV project and a CV project that gets asked about for twenty minutes.

Four layers of importance, and they're worth being able to separate cleanly when someone asks.

**1. The phenomenon is real and expensive.** The term "bullwhip" was popularized after Procter & Gamble noticed that factory orders for Pampers swung wildly while actual babies — the underlying demand — stayed almost perfectly constant. The distortion was manufactured entirely by the supply chain observing itself. You saw the consumer version in 2020: toilet paper vanished, not because people needed dramatically more of it, but because a modest demand bump got amplified into panic orders at every tier. Same in the 2021 chip shortage. Companies carry billions in excess inventory as insurance against a wave they themselves generate.

**2. The cause is structural, not human.** This is the counterintuitive bit, and it's the reason the simulation is interesting rather than obvious. MIT Sloan has run this game with supply chain executives for sixty years, people who *know* the bullwhip exists, and they still produce it. Why? Because if you can only see your immediate customer's orders and your shipments take two weeks to arrive, over-ordering is the *locally rational* move. Every agent optimizes correctly, and the system still fails. That's a systems-thinking lesson that generalizes far past supply chains — it's the same shape as bank runs, traffic jams from a single brake tap, and cascading service timeouts in a microservice mesh.

**3. The AI angle — this is the part that makes it current.** Everyone right now is racing to put LLM agents in charge of real operational decisions: inventory, procurement, pricing, ad budgets. The unexamined assumption is that each agent being individually smart makes the system smart. Your simulation tests that assumption directly and cheaply. If your LLM agents reproduce the bullwhip — and they will — you've demonstrated that **agent intelligence doesn't fix coordination failure, because the failure lives in the information topology, not in the agents.**

That gives you a sentence most candidates can't say:

> "The fix wasn't a better prompt or a smarter model. It was giving every agent visibility into real demand. The architecture mattered more than the intelligence."

For any interviewer thinking about multi-agent systems, that's the answer they're hoping to hear, because it's the failure mode they're actually worried about in production.

**4. What it says about you.** A crew project shows you can wire up a framework. This shows you formed a hypothesis, built a controlled environment, ran a variable ablation, and produced a number. That's engineering, not integration. And the honest scope is refreshing — you can say "this is a toy chain of four agents, I'm not claiming it models Walmart" and still have the finding stand, because the finding is about *mechanism*, not scale.

One caveat worth holding: LLM agents aren't humans, so if yours amplify variance, you've shown that these agents behave this way under these prompts — not that all agents everywhere must. Saying that yourself, before the interviewer says it, is worth more than the result itself.

## Input

There are three different things called "input," which is probably where the confusion is.

**1. Config — what you set once before a run:**

```json
{
  "weeks": 40,
  "demand_pattern": [8,8,8,8,8,12,12,12,...],
  "shipping_delay": 2,
  "message_delay": 1,
  "condition": "chat_enabled"
}
```

That's the only human input. Customer demand is scripted, not generated — it must be boring and known, or you can't attribute the chaos to the agents.

**2. Payload — what one agent receives on one turn.** This is the actual LLM input:

```json
{
  "role": "Wholesaler",
  "week": 12,
  "inventory": 4,
  "backlog": 9,
  "arriving_this_week": 8,
  "order_from_downstream": 22,
  "my_recent_orders": [8, 8, 10, 14, 20],
  "inbox": [
    {"from": "Retailer", "text": "Demand jumped, I'm running dry — push more my way"},
    {"from": "Distributor", "text": "I'm backlogged, your last order will be late"}
  ]
}
```

Note what's *absent*: real customer demand, and every other tier's inventory. The blindness is the experiment.

**3. Response — what the agent returns:**

```json
{
  "order": 30,
  "to_upstream": "Ramping hard, please prioritize my next three orders",
  "to_downstream": "Backlogged nine units, expect delay",
  "reasoning": "Backlog is growing and shipments take two weeks..."
}
```

## ProcessIn code terms, one week is:

```python
for week in range(40):
    engine.deliver_shipments()      # pipeline from 2 weeks ago lands
    engine.deliver_messages()       # inbox from 1 week ago lands
    engine.fill_orders()            # ship min(inventory, order + backlog)
    for tier in chain:              # ← the only LLM calls
        reply = agent(tier).decide(engine.payload_for(tier))
        engine.queue_order(tier, reply["order"])
        engine.queue_messages(tier, reply["to_upstream"], reply["to_downstream"])
    engine.log(week)
```

The engine is plain Python and holds all the truth — inventory, backlog, pipelines. The agents never mutate state; they only return intentions. That separation is what makes results trustworthy, and it's worth saying out loud in the interview: *"the agents can't cheat, because they can't touch the ledger."*

Cost is small — 4 agents × 40 weeks = 160 calls per run. Cheap model, a couple of minutes.

**Messages get a delay too** (1 week, vs 2 for shipping). This matters: instant perfect communication would be a different, less interesting world. Delayed communication is what real supply chains, and real distributed systems, actually have.

## Why messaging makes the project sharper

You now have a genuinely open question instead of a replication:

> Does letting agents talk reduce the bullwhip, or amplify it?

Both answers are defensible before you run it. Talking might share real information and dampen the swings. Or "I'm running dry, push more my way" propagates *fear* faster than goods propagate, and the chain panics harder than it would have in silence. You don't know until you run it — which is the whole reason the simulation exists.

**And here's the finding to hunt for:** compare each agent's outbound message against its actual inventory in the engine log. If an agent says *"I'm critically short"* while sitting on comfortable stock, it has learned to exaggerate to get priority — without you ever prompting it to. Strategic misrepresentation emerging from nothing but a scarcity incentive is a legitimately striking result, and it's trivially detectable because you have ground truth for every claim.

Add one metric next to the amplification ratio: **claim inflation** — how far urgency in messages diverges from real inventory. That single number turns "I built a sim" into "I found something."

## UI change

Add a message layer between the tier cards: small text bubbles sliding between neighbors as the weeks tick, one line each, fading after a beat. When the red line spikes and you can *see* three panicked messages flying upstream in the same week, the causal story tells itself. Keep the full transcript in the JSON log, not on screen — the screen shows only this week's traffic.
